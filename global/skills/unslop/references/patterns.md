# Unslop pattern reference

Use this reference for full rewrites, reviews, or passages that still feel generic after the core pass. Diagnose the function of a phrase before changing it. No word, punctuation mark, sentence length, or grammatical construction is automatically wrong.

## Substance

### Puffery without evidence

**Smell:** "pivotal moment", "testament to", "evolving landscape", "renowned", "stunning", "game-changing".

**Fix:** State the event, mechanism, measured change, or consequence. Keep evaluative language only when the genre calls for it and the text supports it.

### Promotional adjectives doing the noun's job

**Smell:** "robust platform", "powerful solution", "seamless experience", "comprehensive ecosystem".

**Fix:** Say what the platform handles, what the solution prevents, what step disappears, or what the scope includes.

### Vague attribution

**Smell:** "experts believe", "reports suggest", "many users say", "some critics argue".

**Fix:** Name the source and claim. If the source is unavailable, remove the attribution rather than inventing one.

### Name-dropping without relevance

**Smell:** A list of publications, companies, researchers, or frameworks with no explanation of what each contributes.

**Fix:** Keep only the source that matters and state its relevant finding, decision, or evidence.

### Generic challenge-and-triumph framing

**Smell:** "Despite these challenges, the project continues to thrive."

**Fix:** State the actual obstacle and current result. Delete the sentence if it contains neither.

### Generic conclusion

**Smell:** "The future looks bright", "Only time will tell", "This marks an exciting new chapter".

**Fix:** End on the last concrete fact, decision, implication, or next action.

### Feeling in place of information

**Smell:** "keeps data close at hand", "code you can trust", "a delightful workflow".

**Fix:** Name the behavior, latency, guarantee, failure mode, or user-visible change that creates the feeling.

## Language

### AI-associated vocabulary used abstractly

Common suspects include "delve", "leverage", "landscape", "pivotal", "showcase", "tapestry", "testament", "underscore", "vibrant", "interplay", "intricate", and "holistic".

**Fix:** Replace the word only when it is vague, ornamental, or less exact than a plain alternative. Keep technically exact uses such as a geographic landscape, financial leverage, or an accessibility enhancement.

### Fancy substitutes for plain verbs

**Smell:** "serves as", "stands as", "boasts", "utilizes", "facilitates", "enables" where "is", "has", "uses", or a direct action verb says the same thing.

**Fix:** Use the shortest exact verb. Keep the longer verb when it adds a real relationship or capability.

### Superficial `-ing` tail

**Smell:** A sentence ends with "highlighting", "ensuring", "reflecting", "showcasing", or "fostering" followed by a claim that does not logically follow.

**Fix:** Delete the tail, give it a subject and evidence, or make it a separate claim.

### Nominalization

**Smell:** "perform an evaluation of", "make a determination", "provide an explanation".

**Fix:** Use the verb: "evaluate", "decide", "explain".

### "Not just X, but Y"

**Smell:** The construction creates drama but hides the main claim.

**Fix:** State Y directly, then add X only if it matters.

### False range

**Smell:** "from security to developer experience" when the endpoints are not a scale and the sentence merely lists topics.

**Fix:** Name the topics directly.

### Forced group of three

**Smell:** Three adjectives, clauses, examples, or headings chosen for cadence rather than content.

**Fix:** Use the natural number. Keep three when the material genuinely has three parts.

### Synonym cycling

**Smell:** One object becomes "the service", "the platform", "the solution", and "the system" in the same passage.

**Fix:** Pick the correct term and repeat it.

### Hedge chain

**Smell:** "could potentially perhaps", "it may be possible that", "arguably might".

**Fix:** Keep the shortest uncertainty marker that matches the evidence: "may", "likely", "I infer", or a stated confidence level.

### Unsupported intensifier or adverb

**Smell:** "significantly improves", "dramatically reduces", "runs quickly" with no measure or comparison.

**Fix:** Give the measured change, name the comparison, choose a stronger exact verb, or remove the modifier.

### Abstract metaphor jargon

Common suspects include "substrate", "wedge", "vector", "locus", "nexus", "primitive", "bedrock", "scaffolding", "north star", "flywheel", "ratchet", "endgame", and "surface" when used metaphorically.

**Fix:** Name the actual object, mechanism, phase, constraint, or action. Keep established domain terms when they are the precise shared vocabulary.

## Tone and communication artifacts

The governing rules live in "Tone toward the reader" in SKILL.md. These entries are recognition patterns; a fix line appears only where it adds something the rule does not already say.

### Chatbot preamble

**Smell:** "Of course", "Certainly", "Great question", "Let's dive in", "Here's the honest truth".

**Fix:** Start with the answer, result, or requested text.

### Canned closing

**Smell:** "I hope this helps", "Let me know if you need anything else", or a redundant final recap.

**Fix:** Stop after the last needed fact, source, or action.

### Sycophancy

**Smell:** Automatic agreement, praise, validation, or mirroring before analysis.

### Condescension or smart-ass tone

**Smell:** Sarcasm, mockery, scolding, rhetorical put-downs, or language that grades the reader.

### Dismissive difficulty language

**Smell:** "simply", "obviously", "easy", "trivial", "just", or "quickly" used to judge a task or reassure without helping.

**Fix:** Keep the word when it is objectively necessary, such as "a simple linear model".

### Scripted empathy or mind-reading

**Smell:** "I understand how frustrating this must be" when the reader did not say that, or speculation about motives, intelligence, competence, or emotion.

**Fix:** Address the stated problem. Do not invent an emotional state.

### Manufactured first person

**Smell:** "I love", "I have seen", "in my experience", or an anecdote added only to sound human.

**Fix:** Use first person only for something the speaker can truthfully own.

## Structure and formatting

### Restating the request

**Smell:** The answer opens by paraphrasing the user's question without adding anything.

**Fix:** Lead with the answer or deliverable.

### Mechanical sectioning

**Smell:** A heading for every paragraph, a fixed introduction-body-conclusion shape, or sections that exist only to make the response look complete.

**Fix:** Use the least structure that helps the reader navigate.

### Inline-header list

**Smell:** "**Performance:** Performance improved..." or a bold label that repeats the sentence.

**Fix:** Write a normal sentence, or use a bold lead-in that names the subject and is followed by new information.

### Boldface and callout overuse

**Smell:** Every noun, acronym, warning, or conclusion is bold.

**Fix:** Reserve emphasis for navigation or one genuinely important distinction.

### Decorative emoji

**Smell:** Emoji in headings or bullets that carry no information.

**Fix:** Remove them unless the requested voice or brand uses them.

### Title case everywhere

**Smell:** Every heading capitalizes major words regardless of project style.

**Fix:** Prefer sentence case unless the house style says otherwise.

### Punctuation as a crutch

**Smell:** Repeated em dashes, colons, semicolons, parentheses, ellipses, or fragments create the rhythm instead of sentence structure.

**Fix:** Recast some sentences. Keep punctuation that genuinely fits the voice and meaning.

### Uniform rhythm

**Smell:** Every sentence has the same length and syntax, or every sentence is clipped into a fragment.

**Fix:** Let sentence length follow the idea. Vary openings and transitions without adding filler.

### Dense sentence

**Smell:** The reader must backtrack to identify the subject, condition, actor, or reference.

**Fix:** Move the main subject and verb closer, split independent thoughts, or repeat the noun instead of using an ambiguous pronoun.

### Redundant summary

**Smell:** The conclusion repeats the introduction or all preceding bullets.

**Fix:** End on the final implication or action. Keep a summary only when the length, stakes, or requested format justify it.

## Clarity and accessibility

### Hidden actor

**Smell:** Passive voice makes it unclear whether the reader, client, server, compiler, or another actor performs the action.

**Fix:** Name the actor. Keep passive voice when responsibility is irrelevant or should be de-emphasized.

### Buried condition

**Smell:** The reader reaches the end of an instruction before learning whether it applies.

**Fix:** Put the condition or goal first when that improves scanning.

### Inconsistent terminology

**Smell:** Synonyms make one concept look like several.

**Fix:** Use one term for one concept and the repository's real names for symbols, flags, files, and commands.

### Ambiguous pronoun

**Smell:** "it", "this", "they", or "which" could refer to more than one thing or to a whole clause.

**Fix:** Repeat the noun or rewrite the sentence.

### Meaningless link text

**Smell:** "click here", "read more", or a bare URL whose destination is unclear.

**Fix:** Use the page title or a short description that makes sense out of context.

### Positional-only reference

**Smell:** "the button on the right", "the diagram above", or meaning conveyed only through color or shape.

**Fix:** Refer to the named control or section and provide an equivalent textual cue.

### Visual without equivalent text

**Smell:** An image or diagram contains information that prose, alt text, or a caption does not provide.

**Fix:** Add concise equivalent text that states the visual's meaning, not every decorative detail.

## Contexts that override the default

- **Creative writing:** Rhythm, metaphor, ambiguity, repetition, and unusual punctuation may be the point.
- **Marketing:** Promotional language can be appropriate, but claims still need support and the voice must match the brand.
- **Academic, scientific, legal, or policy writing:** Passive voice, formal terminology, and calibrated hedging may be necessary.
- **Quoted or regulated text:** Preserve exact wording unless the user explicitly authorizes changes.
- **Established house style:** Follow its spelling, capitalization, punctuation, and terminology rules.
- **A strong human draft:** Make no change when a rewrite would only normalize the writer's voice.
